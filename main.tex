\heading{Project Summary}
\begin{center}
\textbf{Project: Cloud-Accelerated NeuroFrame: Geometry-Aware Foundation Models for Extracellular Electrophysiology}\\
\textbf{PI: Erdem Varol, New York University}
\end{center}

Most invasive neural recordings are referenced by informal targeting procedures rather than by validated biological coordinates. Two experiments described as the same region may sample different tissue, different local populations, and different molecular environments. This hidden variation reduces reproducibility, and it can make a sampling difference look like a task effect.

We will develop NeuroFrame, a cloud-accelerated program for biologically grounded registration of extracellular recordings. Our immediate goal is to measure how accurately self-supervised models infer atlas position from extracellular signals and probe geometry in held-out animals and recording systems. We will scale geometry-aware local field potential encoders past the present limit of 95 million parameters and one million samples. We will train those models to convergence at larger capacity and corpus size. We will then extend the representation to native 30-kilohertz action potential recordings. Every model will return a predictive distribution rather than a point estimate. We will evaluate with animal-level splits, correspondence-breaking controls, calibration, and abstention when the evidence does not support fine localization.

Google Cloud is necessary for this work. Our longest multi-GPU jobs exceed the single-allocation limits of NYU's Torch cluster, and the action potential corpus needs more memory, local staging, and input-output bandwidth than the present pipeline. We will use public Allen Brain Observatory, IBL Brain-Wide Map, and DANDI data only. The award will produce converged scaling measurements, released checkpoints, reproducible evaluation code, and an interactive atlas demonstration. These results will strengthen future NSF, NIH BRAIN, NeuroNex, and CRCNS proposals.

\heading{Research Project}
\subheading{1. Recordings lack a common biological reference}

Large-scale neural recordings now span many animals, laboratories, tasks, and probe designs. The biological reference for a recording is still often an informal targeting coordinate. Two experiments labeled as the same region may sample different atlas positions, different local populations, or different molecular environments. Pooling such recordings creates a hidden confound. Analyzing every animal separately loses statistical power. Histology remains essential, but it is post hoc and static, and it cannot estimate electrical-source position or tissue motion during a recording.

Our central hypothesis is that the spatial structure of extracellular activity, relative to known probe geometry, carries enough information to estimate biological position at resolutions supported by held-out data. This award will establish the first two technical pillars of that program. The first is atlas registration from local field potentials and probe geometry. The second is a native action potential extension that tests whether the same idea reaches finer spatial and cellular information.

Preliminary work establishes feasibility (Figure~\ref{fig:prelim}). In held-out IBL animals, a geometry-aware encoder with a probe-sequence readout reached 1,290 micrometers of three-dimensional localization error, and 1,200 micrometers after rigid refinement against known contact geometry. Error fell at every pretraining scale from 25,000 to one million samples. Two questions remain open. We have scaled data by a factor of forty while holding capacity fixed at roughly 95 million parameters, and the gain per decade is narrowing. We do not yet know whether that narrowing reflects limited capacity and truncated pretraining or a limit of the signal itself. We also do not know whether native 30-kilohertz modeling recovers information that our current single-channel, downsampled transfer pipeline discards.

\subheading{2. Objectives}
\textbf{Aim 1. Converged capacity and data scaling.} We will train geometry-aware encoders at approximately 95 million, 317 million, and 1 billion parameters, across corpus sizes from the present one million samples toward 10 million and 32 million chunks. We will complete the intended training schedule for every cell rather than reporting the early snapshots that local wall-time limits now force. We will evaluate every headline cell with frozen linear, nonlinear, and sequence readouts in held-out animals and probes.

\textbf{Aim 2. Native action potential feasibility.} We will train a native 30-kilohertz encoder with a multichannel spatial frontend. Our current transfer experiment resamples action potential data to the local field potential rate and uses one channel, and it produced a negative held-out result. That experiment cannot test the spatial waveform information that motivates the approach. We will determine whether native sampling and neighboring-contact context improve source and localization representations, and we will report the negative result if they do not.

\textbf{Aim 3. Molecular context with calibrated registration.} We will propagate atlas-position uncertainty into atlas-derived gene expression and molecular annotation. We will test whether the same electrical representations predict independently measured cell types. For every model we will report raw coordinate error, per-axis error, atlas-region accuracy, predictive uncertainty, calibration, and abstention rate, using complete animal, session, and probe holdouts together with correspondence-breaking controls.

\textbf{Aim 4. Behavior decoding from the same representation.} We will freeze the pretrained representation and fit lightweight readouts for stimulus side, choice, and wheel movement in locked IBL windows, without training a behavior-specific encoder. We will evaluate subject-disjoint and probe-disjoint transfer and compare neural predictions against behavior-only baselines. This tests whether one representation carries behaviorally relevant information while sampling differences are held in view.

\subheading{3. Methods, evaluation, and controls}
\textbf{Data.} We will use public Allen Brain Observatory, IBL Brain-Wide Map, and DANDI resources. Human intracranial recordings are outside the scope of this award and will remain on NYU infrastructure under existing agreements. Manifests will freeze animal, session, and probe membership, preprocessing, probe geometry, labels, exclusions, and splits before final evaluation.

\textbf{Models.} The local field potential model retains our validated masked-prediction, temporal-contrastive, and codebook-diversity objectives. Geometry-aware distance supervision, derived from relative contact layout, is added to them. Absolute channel identifiers and atlas coordinates are never supplied to the encoder. The action potential model uses native 30-kilohertz windows and neighboring-contact context under the same design.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We will evaluate in three stages: held-out animals within represented recording systems, zero-shot transfer to a laboratory or probe family excluded from training, and prespecified few-animal recalibration if zero-shot transfer fails. Independent animals, sessions, probes, or preparations will determine confidence intervals, not windows or contacts. We will make formal coverage claims only for exchangeable calibration and test cohorts. Under declared distribution shift we will report empirical coverage and abstain when overlap diagnostics do not support a calibrated answer. Every stage is checkpointed, and we will record software, hardware, seed, manifest, effective sample size, and cost.

\heading{Research Project (continued)}
\subheading{4. Cloud-enabled work and timeline}
\textbf{August 2026. Portability and efficiency gate.} We will containerize the validated training and evaluation pipeline, stage sharded public corpora on Cloud Storage and node-local SSD, and benchmark mixed precision with fp32 master weights, FlashAttention, gradient accumulation, distributed loading, and FSDP. We will reproduce our current baselines on Google Cloud before launching the grid.

\textbf{September. Capacity and data grid.} We will run the capacity and corpus cells that pass the efficiency gate, using checkpointed multi-GPU jobs long enough to avoid wall-time truncation. The existing one-million-sample column is the baseline. The award funds the larger corpus columns and the capacity comparison.

\textbf{October. Native action potentials and replication.} We will train native models on the H200 configuration, run the highest-priority replications, and evaluate held-out recording systems. We will stop or narrow any arm that fails its prespecified memory, throughput, or control gate.

\textbf{November 1--25. Evaluation and release.} We will freeze checkpoints, run calibration and uncertainty analyses, produce scaling and failure-mode figures, publish manifests and code, and deploy a small interactive atlas demonstration. November 26--30 is reserved for artifact export and account closure. We will start no uncheckpointed experiment in that window.

\subheading{6. Expected outcomes}
The minimum outcome is a converged capacity and data scaling curve, measured rather than extrapolated, and a reproducible answer about whether native action potential modeling adds information beyond local field potentials. A positive result would establish a route to biologically grounded registration across recording systems. A negative result would identify a tested resolution or architecture limit, and would keep the field from reading truncated or downsampled runs as evidence of failure. We will release checkpoints, animal-level manifests, calibration tools, and evaluation code.

\subheading{Why Google Cloud rather than NYU resources}
NYU's Torch cluster serves short and single-GPU jobs well. The workloads in this proposal instead combine multi-GPU training, multi-day uninterrupted execution, high host memory, large local staging, and sustained input-output. Torch allocates either 24 GPUs for 48 hours or 4 GPUs for 168 hours, so no single uninterrupted computation there can exceed 672 GPU-hours. One seed of our largest planned cell, the 1-billion-parameter model at 32 million chunks, needs approximately 2,411 GPU-hours. That is 3.6 times the longest allocation the cluster can grant, and it is why our completed 95-million-parameter run at one million samples, which required approximately 747 GPU-hours, had to be chained across wall-time-limited jobs. Native action potential training additionally needs more memory and staging than the present pipeline. Google Cloud supplies the simultaneous multi-GPU and multi-day window, H200 memory, bundled node-local SSD, and Cloud Batch orchestration that these runs require.

\subheading{Governance and cost control}
We will use containerized jobs, versioned manifests, and least-privilege project access. Logs will record configuration, seed, split, hardware, cost, and artifact checksum. Cloud Storage will hold public Allen, IBL, and DANDI data and the artifacts derived from them. Human intracranial data are excluded. We will stop any job that fails its memory, throughput, or control criteria and redirect its allocation to prespecified evaluation or replication work. Vertex AI managed-training fees, Vizier trials, committed-use contracts, and TPU work are excluded from this request. Results and released checkpoints will be exported to NYU before the credit expires.

\heading{Future Funding Plans}
This award will establish NeuroFrame as a measured and reproducible research program. It will produce three assets that reviewers ask for and that the current allocation shape at NYU cannot reliably deliver. The first is a converged capacity and data scaling curve rather than a set of wall-time-truncated snapshots. The second is an honest native action potential feasibility result, including a well-controlled negative result if the approach does not improve localization. The third is a public release of checkpoints, animal-level manifests, calibration analyses, and correspondence-breaking controls, so that the claims can be tested independently.

The results will strengthen the following submissions.
